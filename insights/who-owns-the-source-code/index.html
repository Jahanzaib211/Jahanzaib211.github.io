Who owns the source code you paid for

ALI LABS X engineering, 24 November 2026

A company pays for a system, uses it for two years, then asks another developer to change it. Only then does it learn that it never received the code, or the right to change it. The time to prevent that is before the first payment.

Payment and ownership are separate

Copyright in software generally begins with whoever created it, and the rules for commissioned work differ between countries. Do not assume that an invoice transfers ownership. Your contract should say who owns what, and when. This article is general information, so have a qualified lawyer review your agreement.

Three parts of one system

  • Work made only for you. This is the part you would normally expect to own.
  • The supplier’s existing tools. You usually receive a licence to use them, so check that it is lasting and lets others maintain your system.
  • Open source components. Nobody can assign these to you. Their licences apply, so ask for a list.

Settle these in writing

  • What you own, and whether ownership passes on payment or on delivery.
  • Your right to modify the system and to appoint another supplier.
  • A list of third party and open source components with their licences.
  • Ownership of your data, always and without conditions.
  • What the supplier may reuse or show to others.

What a real handover contains

  • The complete source code with its history.
  • Instructions to build, install and restore the system.
  • Accounts, domains and keys in your company’s name.
  • A list of known problems.
  • A session where your people install it themselves while the supplier watches.

The last item is the true test. If someone else can install and run the system from what you were given, you have received it.
